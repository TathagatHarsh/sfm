📌 Most common exam mistakes

⚠ Confusing "market risk premium" with "stock's excess return" — MRP is a constant, it doesn't scale with beta.
⚠ Forgetting to multiply (1+g) in terminal value numerator — gives wrong option in MCQ.
⚠ Applying normal IRR rule to financing projects (positive CF first) — the decision reverses.
⚠ Using total risk (SD) vs systematic risk (beta) — CAPM uses beta, scenario analysis uses SD.

What is CAPM?

The Capital Asset Pricing Model gives the expected return an investor should demand to hold a risky asset. The key insight: in a well-diversified portfolio, all firm-specific (unsystematic) risk cancels out. Only market (systematic) risk remains, measured by beta (β).

Core intuition: You only get paid for risks you cannot avoid. Since anyone can eliminate firm-specific risk for free (just diversify), the market won't reward you for it. Beta captures the unavoidable part.

Systematic vs Unsystematic risk

TypeAlso calledExamplesDiversifiable?Rewarded?
SystematicMarket risk, beta riskRecession, interest rate changes, inflationNoYes
UnsystematicFirm-specific, idiosyncraticCEO scandal, product recall, strikeYesNo
Expected Return (Ri) = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf)

Term by term

Rf — Risk-free rate. The return for simply waiting (e.g., 91-day T-bill, Govt bond yield). No risk taken, so no risk premium.

β (beta) — Sensitivity of stock to market movements. β=1 means it moves with the market. β=2 means it's twice as volatile as the market. β=0 means no market correlation.

(Rm − Rf) — Market Risk Premium. The extra reward demanded for holding the market portfolio over risk-free. This is a market-wide constant — it does NOT depend on an individual stock's beta.

Rf=7%, Rm=13%, Small stock return=23%
Market premium = 13%−7% = 6%
Excess return of small stock = 23%−7% = 16%
Beta = 16% ÷ 6% = 2.67
If beta increases by 1 → new β=3.67 → Expected return = 7% + 3.67×6% = 29% → Increase = 26% < 100% (less than a factor of 1)

CAPM Assumptions

Investors are rational and risk-averse — they prefer less risk for same return.
Capital markets are strongly efficient — prices reflect all available information.
Investors are well-diversified — firm-specific risk is eliminated, not compensated.
Investors have homogeneous expectations — Investor A and Investor B have the same risk-return forecast for a stock.
No taxes, transaction costs, or restrictions on short selling.

What beta tells you

BetaInterpretationExample
β = 0No market correlationT-bills, gold sometimes
β = 1Moves exactly with marketMarket index ETF
β < 1Less volatile than marketUtilities, FMCG
β > 1More volatile than marketTech stocks, small caps
β < 0Moves opposite to marketSome gold ETFs, put options
❌ Trap 1: "Beta doubles → MRP doubles"
FALSE. Market Risk Premium = Rm−Rf is a single number for the whole market. It does NOT change with individual stock's beta. What doubles is the stock's risk premium (β × MRP).
❌ Trap 2: "Beta = 2 means stock is 2× as volatile as market"
HALF-TRUE. Beta=2 means the stock's excess returns are twice as volatile as the market's excess returns. The total volatility (standard deviation) comparison is different.
❌ Trap 3: "Beta is the risk of the market"
FALSE. Beta is the market risk OF THE STOCK. The risk of the market itself is the standard deviation of NIFTY 50 (or the index).
❌ Trap 4: Using CAPM in boom/bust scenarios
If a question says "during economic boom, stock gives 1 SD higher return" — this is a scenario using TOTAL risk (SD), NOT CAPM. CAPM is for equilibrium expected returns only.
⚠ Daily vs Annual beta: Daily beta is usually HIGHER than annual beta because short-term noise amplifies measured sensitivity. Over longer periods, returns revert more toward fundamentals.

Discounted Cash Flow Valuation

The intrinsic value of any asset is the present value of all future cash flows it generates, discounted at the appropriate rate (WACC for firm value, cost of equity for equity value). You forecast for an explicit period (usually 3–5 years), then capture everything after via Terminal Value.

Value = Σ [FCFFt / (1+WACC)t] + TV / (1+WACC)n
Key insight: Terminal value typically accounts for 60–80% of the total estimated firm value. This means small errors in the long-run growth assumption (g) can completely dominate the valuation. Handle with extreme care.

Gordon Growth Model — Terminal Value

After the explicit forecast period, we assume cash flows grow forever at a stable perpetual rate g. The TV captures the PV of this infinite stream:

TVat year n = FCFFn × (1 + g) / (WACC − g)
⚠ Critical: The numerator is FCFFn × (1+g) — the NEXT period's cash flow. If the question gives you FCFF at year n and says "grows at g in perpetuity", you must multiply by (1+g). Missing this gives you option C in MCQs (the trap answer).
From your mock paper (Q9):
FCFF 2010 = 23,559 → grows 10%/yr for 3 years
FCFF 2013 = 23,559 × (1.1)³ = 31,357
TV = 31,357 × (1.07) / (0.12 − 0.07) = 33,552 / 0.05 = ₹6,71,040
If you forgot (1+g): 31,357 / 0.05 = 6,27,140 → that's the trap option C

Why terminal growth rate is so sensitive

Mathematical reason: As g approaches WACC, the denominator (WACC−g) → 0, making TV → ∞. A change from g=7% to g=8% when WACC=12% changes the denominator from 0.05 to 0.04 — a 20% change — swinging TV by 25%.
Economic constraint: No company can sustain a growth rate higher than the economy forever. Therefore, terminal g must be ≤ nominal GDP growth rate (typically 5–8% in India).

Net Present Value — the gold standard

NPV directly measures value creation in currency terms. It asks: after discounting all future cash flows at the cost of capital, does the project create or destroy value?

NPV = −C₀ + CF₁/(1+r) + CF₂/(1+r)² + ... + CFₙ/(1+r)ⁿ

Decision rule

NPVDecisionMeaning
NPV > 0AcceptProject earns more than cost of capital → creates value
NPV = 0IndifferentProject earns exactly the cost of capital
NPV < 0RejectProject destroys value — better to invest at market rate
NPV advantage: It directly adds to shareholder wealth. Always reliable. IRR is derived from NPV — use NPV when in doubt.

The IRR Rule

IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV = 0. It represents the project's own internal rate of return. The standard rule: Accept if IRR > Cost of Capital.

Why this works for normal projects: If the project's return (IRR) exceeds what investors demand (WACC), the NPV will be positive. So IRR>WACC ↔ NPV>0 for conventional cash flows.
Financing project REVERSAL: When cash INFLOWS come first (e.g., a construction advance received upfront) and OUTFLOWS come later, the NPV profile slopes UPWARD with discount rate. The rule reverses: Accept only if IRR < WACC.

When IRR Cannot Be Applied

Case 1 — Financing project (inflows before outflows):
Cash flows: +C₀, −CF₁, −CF₂ ... The NPV curve is inverted. IRR>WACC now means NPV<0. Decision reverses to: Accept if IRR<WACC.
Case 2 — Multiple sign changes (Multiple IRR problem):
If cash flows change sign more than once (e.g., −,+,−,+), by Descartes' Rule there can be as many positive IRRs as sign changes. The NPV curve crosses zero multiple times. No single IRR to use → IRR rule is inapplicable.
Solution when IRR fails: Use NPV. Always reliable. Or use Modified IRR (MIRR) which assumes reinvestment at WACC instead of at IRR.

DuPont Analysis

ROE can be split into three drivers: profitability (how much profit per rupee of sales), efficiency (how much sales per rupee of assets), and leverage (how many rupees of assets per rupee of equity). This lets you diagnose why ROE changed.

ROE = Net Profit Margin × Asset Turnover × Equity Multiplier

What each driver means

DriverFormulaWhat it captures
Net Profit MarginNet Income / SalesOperating efficiency, pricing power, cost control
Asset TurnoverSales / Total AssetsAsset efficiency — how hard assets are working
Equity MultiplierTotal Assets / EquityFinancial leverage — higher EM means more debt
Warning: A high ROE driven by high Equity Multiplier is not necessarily good — it means high leverage, which amplifies both returns and bankruptcy risk.

Computing Equity Multiplier from ROE

Given ROE, NPM, and ATO, rearrange: EM = ROE / (NPM × ATO)

From your mock paper (Q6):
2021: NPM=10%, ATO=1.2, ROE=15%
EM₂₀₂₁ = 0.15 / (0.10 × 1.2) = 0.15 / 0.12 = 1.25

2024: NPM=20% (doubled), ATO=1.2 (unchanged), ROE=25%
EM₂₀₂₄ = 0.25 / (0.20 × 1.2) = 0.25 / 0.24 = 1.04

Total % change = (1.04 − 1.25) / 1.25 = −16.8%
This is over 4 years → Average annual = −16.8% / 4 = −4.2% per year
Interpretation: Despite ROE rising from 15% to 25%, the firm actually de-leveraged (EM fell). The ROE improvement was driven entirely by margin expansion and maintained by the same asset turnover.

Free Cash Flow to Firm (FCFF)

FCFF is the cash generated by the business after covering operating costs and essential reinvestments, but before any payments to capital providers (both debt and equity). It is available to ALL stakeholders — hence "to Firm".

Why use FCFF? In DCF valuation, we want to capture what the business generates irrespective of how it's financed. Using FCFF with WACC as the discount rate gives the total firm value (equity + debt).

EBIT Method (most common in exams)

FCFF = EBIT × (1 − Tax Rate) + Depreciation − ΔNWC − Capex
Step by step:
1. Start with EBIT (earnings before interest and tax)
2. Apply tax shield: EBIT × (1−t) = NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax)
3. Add back Depreciation (non-cash charge — wasn't actually paid out)
4. Subtract increase in NWC (cash tied up in working capital)
5. Subtract Capex (cash spent on new assets)
From your mock paper (Q8):
EBIT 2010 = ₹24,383 (from income statement)
Tax rate = Income Tax / Pretax Income = 5,678 / 16,223 = 35%
Depreciation 2010 = ₹7,710
No NWC requirement (given) → ΔNWC = 0
FCFF = 24,383 × (1−0.35) + 7,710 = 15,849 + 7,710 = ₹23,559
⚠ Common mistake: Some students use Net Income instead of EBIT. If you use Net Income, you must ADD BACK Interest×(1−t) to get FCFF. With EBIT, you don't need to — interest is already excluded.

Reinvestment Rate & Sustainable Growth

Why subtract NWC increase in cash flows: When a project/firm grows, it needs more inventory, gives more credit (AR rises), makes more provisions. These resources are tied up and unavailable to investors → subtract from free cash flow.
Why add NWC increase in reinvestment rate: Reinvestment = Capex + ΔNWC − Depreciation. It represents total cash being funnelled back into the business to support future growth. NWC increase is part of this reinvestment. More reinvestment → higher sustainable growth, but lower current FCFF.
Reinvestment Rate = (Capex − Depreciation + ΔNWC) / EBIT×(1−t)
Growth rate linkage: g = ROC × Reinvestment Rate
Higher reinvestment → higher sustainable growth, but lower near-term FCFF. There's always a tradeoff.

Net Working Capital — The Logic

NWC = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. In project finance, NWC represents resources tied up in day-to-day operations.

Project starts: Firm buys inventory, sells on credit (AR rises), makes advance payments. Cash is locked in operations → ΔNWC is positive → subtract from FCFF.
Project ends: AR is collected, inventory sold off, payables settled. Locked cash is released back → NWC recovers → add back as cash inflow in terminal year.
Change in NWC = NWC(this year) − NWC(last year)
In reinvestment rate: ΔNWC is added because it represents cash going INTO the business to support higher future sales. It reduces current FCFF but enables future growth.

Quick example from Q15

NWC = 1% of revenue each year.
Year 1: Revenue=10,000 → NWC=100 → ΔNWC = 100 (subtract)
Year 2: Revenue=11,000 → NWC=110 → ΔNWC = 10 (subtract)
Year 4: Project ends → all NWC recovered → +total NWC as inflow
